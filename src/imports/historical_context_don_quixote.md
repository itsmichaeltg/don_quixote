# Historical Context of Don Quixote (1550–1650)

To understand *Don Quixote* (published in 1605 and 1615), one must look at a Spain transitioning from the "Golden Age" of imperial dominance to a period of deep disillusionment. Cervantes wasn't just writing a parody of knight-errantry; he was writing the eulogy for an era.

## Key Historical Events and Influences

### 1. The Battle of Lepanto (1571)
This was a massive naval battle between the Holy League and the Ottoman Empire.
* **The Influence:** Miguel de Cervantes fought in this battle and lost the use of his left hand. The "Soldier’s Tale" within the novel mirrors his own experiences of war and capture, contrasting sharply with the idealized, bloodless battles found in chivalric romances.

### 2. The Reign of Philip II & The Counter-Reformation (1556–1598)
Spain was the champion of Catholicism, characterized by strict religious orthodoxy and the Inquisition.
* **The Influence:** The atmosphere of censorship is felt throughout the novel. When the priest and barber "purge" Quixote’s library, it is a direct satire of the Inquisition’s Index of Prohibited Books. Quixote’s "madness" served as a mask that allowed Cervantes to comment on a rigid society safely.

### 3. The Defeat of the Spanish Armada (1588)
The destruction of Spain’s "Invincible" fleet by England and North Sea storms marked a turning point in Spanish power.
* **The Influence:** This sparked a national shift from imperial confidence to *desengaño* (disillusionment). Quixote’s frequent defeats—expecting glory but receiving beatings—parallel the bruised ego of the declining Spanish Empire.

### 4. The Expulsion of the Moriscos (1609–1614)
King Philip III ordered the mass deportation of the *Moriscos* (Muslims who had converted to Christianity).
* **The Influence:** This is explicitly addressed via the character **Ricote**, a Morisco neighbor of Sancho Panza. Cervantes treats Ricote with empathy, highlighting the human cost of state-mandated exile and the complexity of Spanish identity.

### 5. Economic Inflation and the "Price Revolution"
The influx of silver from the Americas devalued currency and crippled domestic industry, leading to widespread poverty.
* **The Influence:** This explains the gritty setting. Quixote wanders through a landscape of dusty inns and bankrupt gentry. His noble fantasies are constantly interrupted by the harsh economic reality of a country that was "rich" in silver but poor in stability.

### 6. The Rise of the Picaresque Novel
The publication of *Lazarillo de Tormes* (1554) introduced "anti-heroes"—low-born rogues (*pícaros*) struggling to survive.
* **The Influence:** *Don Quixote* is a dialogue between the old Chivalric tradition (high-born heroes) and the new Picaresque tradition (low-born realism). Sancho Panza serves as the grounded, cynical foil to Quixote’s lofty idealism.
